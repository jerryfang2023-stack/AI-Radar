And it will also show up in ways that are hard to measure in GDP immediately. You could bring the world’s greatest superintelligence to many workflows, and still be bound by the laws of corporate physics: getting data prepared and put into a pipeline, process reengineering and change management, aligning on how the new workflow should function, and so on. The upside is that there’s a tremendous amount of opportunity in building the bridges between superintelligence and real-world workflows.

原始观点/摘要：this is a very good and well designed report however, two impt assumptions in it that significantly change the analysis once you start to play with it: 1. the report assumes that jobs can be decomposed into tasks 2. the report models human tasks created by automation always as a fraction of automated tasks. in other words, the underlying assumptions is automation always reduces human labor (or at best leaves it the same) taken in reverse order: does automation always reduce human work? in my experience, the answer is no. automation often creates multiples more work for humans. i have a full argument here (https://t.co/Lb7SUCe2pO) for why this is structurally true. but i think any honest analysis of the economic impacts of AI has to take work creation seriously. can jobs be decomposed into tasks? yes, that is a useful lens. but lists of tasks is a way of looking at jobs’one that makes the question of how much of a job is automatable actually tractable. therefore, it's useful. but this is not the full picture, it's just jobs as exposed to our method of questioning. a good example: under this model, where do the new tasks come from? are they just from bosses that add a list to the task list for workers to complete? where do those tasks come from? unless you can model that well you are missing a key term in the analysis (see the first point above.) another way of looking at this, borrowing from wittgenstein and heidegger, is that a job is a particular way of seeing and caring about the world. an editor notices things a nurse doesn’t, and vice versa. what you care about shapes what you notice, what seems worth doing, and what counts as doing it well. you can make some of that explicit as a task list. but as your tools and circumstances change, the same cares can generate entirely new tasks. that process is part of the job. the above is completely missing from this analysis (understandably because it's, by definition, very hard to model!) but if you take it seriously, it shoudl substantially change your perspective automation and jobs
